It's your second week at Brightcart, a fast-growing online marketplace. Today the whole thing runs on one beefy VM: the web app, the session store (in process memory) and a Postgres database on its own box. On a normal evening it serves about 400 requests per second at 35% CPU.

Twenty times that is 8,000 req/s. You have ten hours.

M

Morgan · Staff SRE · 14 years on-call

I've watched three launches like this go sideways. Every time, the team asked "how do we handle more traffic?" when the real question was "what breaks first, and what happens to users when it does?" Keep that in your head. Your call.

Decision 1 · T-minus 10 hours

How do you prepare for 20x?

The resize needs a reboot, so you take a 6-minute outage at 16:00. Fine. At 00:00:04 the traffic arrives and you're at 100% CPU within seconds. A machine 4x bigger buys about 4x the capacity, and you needed 20x. Worse, one box means one failure domain: when it falls over, everything falls over.

That's vertical scaling. It's simple and often the right first move, but it has a hard ceiling and makes the box a single point of failure.

Capacity looks great on paper. But sessions live in each server's memory, so when the load balancer sends a user's next request to a different server, they're suddenly logged out with an empty cart. Support gets 900 tickets that say "my cart disappeared at checkout."

Horizontal scaling only works when the tier is stateless. You scaled out without moving the state out first.

Exactly the order a veteran would take: first make the tier stateless, then duplicate it. Any server can now handle any request, so adding servers adds capacity, and losing one costs you 1/12th of capacity instead of the whole site.

Morgan nods: "Good. Now the app tier isn't the thing that breaks first. So what is?"

Whatever happened earlier, by 23:30 you have 12 stateless app servers behind a load balancer with sessions in Redis. Autoscaling is on: add instances when average CPU passes 70%. A new instance takes about 4 minutes to boot and warm up.

00:00:03 requests/s 16,800 (normal: 400)
00:00:41 app-cpu-avg 97% autoscaler: launching +12 instances (ETA 4m)
00:01:10 postgres connections 498/500 p99 query 9.4s
00:01:12 SEV1 checkout error rate 31%

It's 42x, not 20x. The influencer posted early and bots joined in. Checkout is failing for a third of users, and the database is drowning.

Decision 2 · 00:01:15

What do you do right now?

Postgres runs one OS process per connection. At 3,000 connections it spends its time context-switching and fighting over locks instead of running queries. Throughput drops. Then the 12 new app servers come online and open even more connections. The database stops responding entirely at 00:04.

More concurrency against a saturated resource makes it slower, not faster. Scaling the stateless tier just moved the bottleneck down a layer.

Autoscaling is reactive. It saw the spike 40 seconds in and needs 4 more minutes. Meanwhile overloaded servers start failing their health checks, the load balancer pulls them out, and the survivors get even more traffic. By the time the reinforcements arrive, the bottleneck is the database, which autoscaling doesn't touch. You survive, but 6 minutes of a third of checkouts failing makes the news.

That's admission control: you can't create capacity in 60 seconds, but you can decide who gets the capacity you have. Error rate drops to 0.4% within a minute. Users see "You're #18,204 in line, about 6 min" instead of a spinning checkout, and the bots time out of the queue.

Morgan: "A slow 'yes' is worth more than a fast 'error'. Shed load deliberately, or the system will shed it for you, at random."

The drop sells out by 00:22. Next morning's postmortem notes that the orders table grew 9x overnight and the database is the obvious next bottleneck. The team agrees to shard orders across 8 Postgres instances. A teammate asks you to pick the shard key.

Decision 3 · The morning after

How do you split the orders across 8 shards?

Surnames aren't evenly distributed. In the US, the "S" shard holds about 10% of customers on its own, while "Q", "X" and "Z" barely register. You've built a hotspot: one shard melts while the others idle. Rebalancing means migrating live data across shards.

Plenty of textbooks use this exact example. It's a classic anti-pattern.

Next month's drop is a single product, so 100% of the writes go to one shard. This is the hot partition problem (aka the celebrity problem). A shard key has to spread the writes during your peak traffic pattern, not your average one.

A hash spreads customers evenly, and one customer's orders stay on one shard, so "my orders" is a single-shard query. The queries you gave up (cross-customer, per-product) move to a system built for them. That's the trade-off senior engineers make on purpose: pick the key for the hot path, and serve everything else asynchronously.

Bonus: hash into many virtual partitions (say 1,024) mapped onto 8 servers, so adding a 9th server means moving partitions instead of rehashing everything.

Debrief

What you just learned

Every concept below showed up in your night. That's the point: you'll remember the session that logged everyone out longer than a definition.

Vertical vs. horizontal scaling

Vertical (scale up)Horizontal (scale out)
WhatBigger machine: more CPU and RAMMore machines behind a load balancer
CeilingHard limit: the biggest box on the marketPractically unbounded
FailureSingle point of failureLose one node, lose 1/N of capacity
Cost of entryNear zero. No code changesThe tier must be stateless first
WhenEarly on, and for databases (until you can't)Web and app tiers, always. Data tiers once you outgrow one box

The AKF Scale Cube: three ways to scale

  • X-axis, duplication: run N copies of the same thing. You did this with 12 stateless app servers.
  • Y-axis, functional split: break the system up by function (checkout, search, catalog) so each part scales on its own. That's how microservices are born.
  • Z-axis, data partitioning (sharding): split the data so each node owns a slice. You did this with the orders table, and saw how much the key matters.

From The Art of Scalability by Abbott & Fisher.

Elasticity, and why autoscaling isn't magic

Elasticity is adding and removing capacity automatically as demand changes. It saves money on quiet nights, but it's reactive: there's a lag to detect the spike plus a lag to boot and warm up instances. For spikes you can see coming:

  • Scheduled scaling: pre-warm to the expected peak 30 minutes before a known event.
  • Warm pools: keep pre-booted instances ready so scaling takes seconds instead of minutes.
  • Admission control / load shedding: a waiting room, rate limits, or dropping low-priority work. Your last line of defense.
  • Know your bottleneck chain: app, then connection pool, then database. Scaling one layer moves the bottleneck down to the next.

It happened for real

Real postmortems

Niantic + Google Cloud · Pokémon GO launchJuly 2016

The team planned for 1x player traffic, with a worst case of about 5x. Real traffic hit 50x the original target. They survived by scaling horizontally on Google's container infrastructure in real time, including a mid-launch load balancer migration.

Lesson: your "worst case" estimate is a guess. Design so that capacity can be added, rather than trying to predict it exactly.

Source: Google Cloud: Bringing Pokémon GO to life

Ticketmaster · Taylor Swift Eras Tour presaleNov 2022

Ticketmaster reported 3.5 billion total system requests, 4x its previous peak, driven by bots and by fans without presale codes. It estimated that about 15% of site interactions hit errors, and some fans lost tickets already in their carts. The general public sale was cancelled.

Lesson: a waiting room only works if admission is strict and bots are filtered before they consume capacity. Demand from bots is still demand on your servers.

Source: Rolling Stone coverage of Ticketmaster's statement

Slack · First workday of the yearJan 4, 2021

Traffic returning after the holidays overloaded an AWS Transit Gateway. Packet loss made web servers wait on the network, so CPU dropped and the autoscaler scaled the web tier down in the middle of the incident. Then it swung to a massive scale-up, and the internal provisioning service hit its own limits (including the Linux open-files limit). Many new instances never finished provisioning.

Lesson: autoscaling is a control loop fed by metrics, so when the metrics lie, it makes things worse. And the system that adds capacity needs capacity of its own.

Source: Slack Engineering: Slack's Outage on January 4th 2021

Field notes

Morgan's rules of thumb

  1. Make it stateless before you make it many.
  2. Every layer you scale pushes the bottleneck to the next one. Know the whole chain: LB → app → pool → DB → disk.
  3. Choose shard keys for your peak traffic pattern. Ask "what happens when one key goes viral?"
  4. Autoscaling handles gradual change. Known spikes need pre-warming, and unknown spikes need load shedding.
  5. A waiting room is a feature, not a failure. A queue position beats a 500 error.

Your turn

Design challenge

Brightcart is launching in Japan. A partnership puts the app on the home screen of a TV shopping channel. Every time a product appears on air (about 40 times a night), traffic spikes 30x for exactly 90 seconds, then drops back to normal.

Design the scaling strategy. What does autoscaling do with a 90-second spike? What do you pre-warm? Where do you shed load?

Hints
  • A 4-minute boot time is longer than the whole 90-second spike. Reactive scaling is useless here.
  • The broadcast schedule is known in advance. Use it.
  • What's cacheable? Product pages for the on-air item are identical for everyone.
What a strong answer covers
  • Scheduled scaling driven by the broadcast schedule (pre-warm before each segment, or hold peak capacity through the show block).
  • Pushing the on-air product page to a CDN or edge cache, so most of the spike never reaches origin.
  • Inventory reservation as the real bottleneck: a queue or token system for "add to cart" on the featured item.
  • Admission control as the safety net, plus a cost comparison of holding capacity for the 3-hour show versus autoscaling.
