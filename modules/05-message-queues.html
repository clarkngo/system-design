Everyone Got Charged Twice

  • Role: primary on-call, payments
  • 3 decisions
  • ~15 min
Sat 11:32 SEV1 #support-escalations: 47 tickets in 20 min: "I was charged twice"
Sat 11:32 payments-worker p50 duration: 24.1s (was 1.8s) payment-provider latency: degraded
Sat 11:33 sqs orders-to-charge visible: 3,412 in-flight: 640 ApproximateReceiveCount > 1: 38%

When a customer clicks "Place order", the checkout API writes the order and publishes an OrderPlaced message to a queue. A fleet of payment workers pulls messages, charges the card through the payment provider, and deletes the message. Separate consumers send the receipt email and update analytics.

The queue's visibility timeout is 20 seconds: once a worker receives a message, it's hidden from other workers for 20 seconds. If it isn't deleted by then, the queue assumes the worker died and hands the message to someone else.

The payment provider is slow today. Charges are taking 24 seconds.

M

Morgan · Staff SRE · 14 years on-call

Do the math out loud. 24 seconds of work, 20 seconds of invisibility. What happens at second 21? And remember: every minute we spend thinking, more people get double-charged. Stop the bleeding first.

Decision 1 · 11:34

Stop the double charges. Now.

The duplicates stop, and so do 3,412 legitimate orders. Those customers see "Order placed" but are never charged, the warehouse never ships, and reconstructing them from database records takes the team all weekend. Never destroy a queue's contents to fix a consumer problem.

More workers means more messages in flight past their timeout, so more duplicate charges, and you're hammering an already-degraded payment provider, which slows it down further. Adding consumers doesn't help when the bottleneck is downstream.

This is the superpower of a queue: decoupling. Checkout keeps accepting orders, the queue durably buffers them, and nobody gets charged at all until you're ready. Customers see "Order placed" and the charge happens 30 minutes later. Nobody notices. The bleeding stops at 11:35.

Bleeding stopped. Now the real fix. The obvious bug is the 20-second timeout versus 24-second work. But Morgan asks a harder question: "Suppose the worker charges the card successfully, then crashes before it deletes the message. What happens?"

Decision 2 · 11:50

What's the real fix?

Necessary, but not enough. A worker can still crash, be OOM-killed or be redeployed after charging and before deleting the message, and the queue will redeliver it. With standard queues, duplicates are part of the contract. You've made them rarer, not impossible.

Now redelivery is harmless. If the same order_id arrives twice, the provider recognizes the idempotency key and returns the original charge instead of creating a new one. "Exactly-once" in practice means at-least-once delivery + idempotent processing. Raising the timeout as well cuts down on wasted work.

Read the fine print. Exactly-once features (Kafka transactions, SQS FIFO deduplication windows) cover the broker's own bookkeeping: deduplicating sends within a window, or atomically consuming and producing within Kafka. None of them can make an external side effect like charging a card happen exactly once. Your consumer still needs to be idempotent.

Workers are back on and the backlog drains. Then, at 12:40:

12:40 payments-worker: TypeError: Cannot read properties of null (reading 'currency') msg 7f3a…
12:40 payments-worker: TypeError: Cannot read properties of null (reading 'currency') msg 7f3a…
12:41 payments-worker: TypeError: … msg 7f3a… (receive count: 41)

A malformed order from a buggy partner integration crashes every worker that picks it up. Then it becomes visible again, gets picked up, and crashes another worker. It's a poison message.

Decision 3 · 12:42

How should the system handle messages that can never succeed?

The worker stops crashing. Two weeks later, a payment-provider outage makes every charge throw for 10 minutes, and your handler deletes 2,800 legitimate orders. Silent data loss is the worst failure mode there is: nothing pages you until customers ask where their stuff is.

The poison message gets parked instead of looping or vanishing. A human (or a replay tool) inspects the dead-letter queue, fixes the data, and redrives it. Retries with backoff handle transient errors, and the dead-letter queue catches permanent ones. Always alert on DLQ depth above zero.

It never will: the data is malformed. Meanwhile it consumes worker time forever, and if this were a FIFO queue or a Kafka partition it would block everything behind it. Ordered streams stall completely on one bad message.

Debrief

What you just learned

Why queues exist

  • Decoupling: checkout doesn't know or care that payments is down. (Decision 1.)
  • Load leveling: a 10x burst becomes a backlog that drains at a steady rate, not a 10x load on downstream services.
  • Asynchronous work: return "Order placed" in 80ms and charge the card in the background.
  • Fan-out: one event, many independent consumers (payments, email, analytics).

Delivery guarantees, honestly

GuaranteeHowRiskUse for
At-most-onceAck before processingLost messagesMetrics, logs you can afford to drop
At-least-onceAck after processingDuplicatesThe default for almost everything
"Exactly-once"At-least-once + idempotent consumerYou have to build itMoney, inventory, anything with side effects

Queue vs. log

Queue (SQS, RabbitMQ)Log (Kafka, Kinesis, Pulsar)
ModelMessage consumed once, then deletedAppend-only. Consumers track their own offset
Fan-outThrough pub/sub topics fanned into queuesNative: any number of consumer groups
ReplayNo (once it's deleted, it's gone)Yes. Rewind the offset and reprocess
OrderingBest-effort, or FIFO per groupStrict per partition
Best forTask and job distributionEvent streams, event sourcing, data pipelines

The dual-write trap and the outbox pattern

Checkout does two things: INSERT order into the database and publish OrderPlaced to the queue. If the process crashes between them, you get an order that's never charged, or a charge for an order that doesn't exist. The fix is the transactional outbox: write the order and an outbox row in the same database transaction, and have a separate relay publish outbox rows to the queue (at-least-once, which your idempotent consumer is ready for).

It happened for real

Real-world stories

Slack · Job queue outage~2016 · write-up published Dec 2017

Slack's job queue was Redis-backed. When database contention slowed job execution, jobs piled up until Redis hit its memory limit. With no free memory, Slack could no longer enqueue new jobs, so every operation that depended on the queue failed. Worse, dequeuing also needed a little free memory, so the queue stayed locked up even after the database recovered, and needed extensive manual intervention. Slack put Kafka in front of Redis as a durable, disk-backed buffer.

Lesson: a queue's job is to absorb backlog. If the backlog can exhaust the queue itself, your shock absorber becomes the point of failure. Know your queue's capacity limits and alert on depth.

Source: Slack Engineering: Scaling Slack's Job Queue

Stripe · Idempotency keysEngineering practice · 2017

Stripe's API lets clients send an Idempotency-Key header with any write. The server stores the key with the result, so a retried request (after a timeout, a crash or a dropped connection) returns the original result instead of charging twice. Stripe's engineering blog explains why this is the only safe way to retry payments over an unreliable network.

Lesson: on a network, you can't tell "failed" from "succeeded but the reply was lost." Idempotency makes that question irrelevant. You'll design these yourself in Incident 07.

Source: Stripe: Designing robust and predictable APIs with idempotency

Field notes

Morgan's rules of thumb

  1. Assume every message arrives at least twice. Design every consumer to be idempotent.
  2. Visibility timeout must exceed worst-case processing time, with margin.
  3. Every queue gets a dead-letter queue. Every DLQ gets an alert.
  4. Never purge a queue to fix a consumer. Pause the consumer instead.
  5. Writing to the database and a queue is a dual write. Use an outbox.

Your turn

Design challenge

Brightcart's order pipeline: once an order is placed, it must be charged, then inventory reserved, then a shipping label created, then the customer emailed. Any step can fail. If the charge succeeds but inventory is gone, the customer must be refunded.

Design it with queues or events. How do you handle a failure in step 3? How do you make sure the email isn't sent twice? How do you see where a given order is?

Hints
  • Look up the saga pattern: a sequence of local transactions, each with a compensating action.
  • Choreography (services react to each other's events) vs. orchestration (one coordinator drives the steps).
What a strong answer covers
  • A saga with compensations (refund after a failed reserve), orchestrated by a state machine or workflow engine like Temporal or Step Functions.
  • Idempotency keys at each step, and an outbox for each service's events.
  • DLQs plus alerting, and an order-state table for visibility.
  • Emails deduplicated by a (order_id, email_type) key.
