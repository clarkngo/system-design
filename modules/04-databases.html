Brightcart runs a single Postgres primary with one async replica. Orders, users, products and inventory all live there. It handles about 3,000 writes per second at peak, comfortably.

M

Morgan · Staff SRE · 14 years on-call

"SQL vs NoSQL" is the wrong question. The right one is: what are the access patterns, and what are the invariants? An invariant is something that must never be false, like "we never sell more units than we have." Find those first.

Decision 1 · Architecture review

Where should inventory and clickstream data live?

Six months of migration later, the first flash sale oversells by 212 units. "Decrement stock and create the order" used to be one ACID transaction. Now it spans two documents, and two concurrent buyers both read stock = 1. You traded away the invariant to solve a scale problem you didn't actually have: 3k writes/s is easy for Postgres.

Inventory stays safe. But 50k clickstream inserts per second fill up the write-ahead log, bloat the indexes and add replication lag. Checkout latency doubles during peak browsing. Low-value, high-volume writes are now starving your most important transactions. It works for a few months.

Polyglot persistence: pick the store by access pattern. Inventory needs transactions and constraints (CHECK (stock >= 0) and SELECT … FOR UPDATE). Clickstream is append-only, time-ordered, rarely updated and tolerant of eventual consistency, which is exactly what wide-column stores and logs are built for. Each system does what it's good at.

Month 4, week 2. Dashboards and "My orders" pages are hammering the primary, so the team routes all reads to the replica. Within a day, support tickets arrive:

Wed 12:14 ticket #88213: "I placed an order and it's not in My Orders. So I ordered again. Now I have two."
Wed 12:15 pg-replica-1 replication lag: 2.8s (peak 11s)
Decision 2 · Replica lag

How do you fix "my order disappeared"?

The bug is gone, but every write now pays a network round-trip to the replica. Worse, when the replica goes down for maintenance, the primary can't commit writes (unless you configure a fallback). You fixed a read problem by making writes slower and more fragile.

Only the user who just wrote needs to see that write immediately. Everyone else can tolerate a few seconds of lag. You keep about 98% of reads on replicas and fix the bug. A more precise version: store the write's log position (LSN) in the session and only read from a replica that has caught up to it.

Users don't read banners. Duplicate orders continue at about 40 a day, each one a refund, a support ticket and a customer who trusts you a little less. A consistency problem doesn't go away because you documented it.

Month 4, Friday, 23:20. The replica has fallen hopelessly behind after a bulk import, so it has to be re-seeded from scratch. You're pairing with Sam over Zoom. Sam has two terminals open: db-primary and db-replica. Both prompts look identical: postgres@db:~$.

Sam: "I'll wipe the replica's data directory and re-seed. We have nightly backups to S3 anyway, so we're safe. Ready?"

Decision 3 · 23:24, finger hovering over Enter

What do you say?

Sam runs rm -rf /var/lib/postgresql/data in the wrong terminal. Ctrl+C after two seconds. The primary is gone.

You open S3. The newest backup is 19 days old. The backup cron job had been failing since a Postgres version upgrade, and the failure emails went to a distribution list that silently rejected them. You restore from a disk snapshot that happens to be 6 hours old. Brightcart loses 6 hours of orders.

Good instinct. That snapshot saves you when the wrong terminal gets wiped. But you only got lucky: nobody had tested whether the nightly backups actually restore, and they don't. Next time there won't be a fresh snapshot.

Sam runs hostname. The terminal they were about to use says db-primary. Silence on the call.

Then you check the backups: 19 days old, failing silently. You postpone the re-seed, fix the backup job, run a test restore to a scratch instance, set the shell prompt on production hosts to red with the hostname, and add a "last successful restore" alert. Nobody outside the team ever hears about tonight. That's what good looks like.

What you just learned

SQL vs. NoSQL: choose by access pattern

Relational (SQL)DocumentWide-columnKey-value
ExamplesPostgres, MySQLMongoDB, DynamoDB*Cassandra, ScyllaDB, BigtableRedis, DynamoDB*
StrengthTransactions, joins, constraints, ad-hoc queriesFlexible nested records, read a whole entity at onceMassive write throughput, time-series, scale-outSimple lookups at extreme speed
WeaknessHarder to scale writes past one primaryCross-document invariantsYou must design tables around your queriesNo queries beyond get/put
Brightcart fitOrders, inventory, paymentsProduct catalogClickstream, activity feedsSessions, carts, cache

* DynamoDB is a key-value and document hybrid.

ACID in one line each

  • Atomicity: all of the transaction happens, or none of it. (No order without the stock decrement.)
  • Consistency: constraints always hold. (stock >= 0)
  • Isolation: concurrent transactions don't see each other's half-done work. (Two buyers can't both take the last unit.)
  • Durability: once committed, it survives a crash.

Scaling a database, in the order you should reach for it

  1. Indexes: turn full-table scans into lookups. Each one costs write speed and storage, so index what you query.
  2. Caching: see Incident 03.
  3. Read replicas: scale reads. Async replicas lag, so plan for read-your-writes. Sync replicas make writes slower and couple availability.
  4. Vertical scaling: a bigger primary. Boring and very effective.
  5. Functional split: move different domains to separate databases.
  6. Sharding: split rows across primaries by a shard key (see Incident 01). It scales writes, but makes cross-shard queries and transactions much harder. Reach for it last.

Backups: RPO, RTO and Schrödinger's backup

  • RPO (recovery point objective): how much data can you afford to lose? Continuous WAL archiving gets you minutes. Nightly dumps get you up to 24 hours.
  • RTO (recovery time objective): how long can you be down while you restore?
  • A backup you haven't restored is a hope, not a backup. Automate test restores and alert on "last successful restore".
  • Replication is not a backup. It faithfully copies your DROP TABLE to every replica.

It happened for real

Real postmortems

GitLab.com · Database outageJan 31, 2017 · ~6 hours of data lost

While fighting replication lag late at night, an engineer ran a command to wipe the secondary's data directory and re-seed it, in a terminal connected to the primary. They stopped it within a second or two, but about 300 GB of production data was already gone. When the team turned to backups, they found that "the process of both finding and using backups failed completely." They restored from an LVM snapshot taken about 6 hours earlier, the best option they had, and livestreamed the recovery.

Lesson: nearly every safeguard can quietly stop working. Test restores, make production prompts unmistakable, and check the hostname before running anything destructive. Decision 3 was modeled on this night.

Source: GitLab: Postmortem of database outage of January 31

Discord · MongoDB → Cassandra → ScyllaDB2015 – 2023

Discord launched on a single MongoDB replica set, deliberately planning to migrate later. As message volume exploded, they moved to Cassandra in 2017 (12 nodes, billions of messages). By 2022 it was 177 nodes holding trillions of messages, with frequent pages and hot partitions (busy channels) causing latency across the cluster. They migrated to ScyllaDB on 72 nodes, and historical-message p99 dropped from 40–125ms to about 15ms.

Lesson: start with what lets you ship, and design for the migration you know is coming. Choose the next database for the access pattern you actually have (append-heavy, time-ordered messages), and expect hot keys.

Sources: How Discord Stores Billions of Messages · How Discord Stores Trillions of Messages

Field notes

Morgan's rules of thumb

  1. Find your invariants first. If you need transactions, you need a database that has them.
  2. Postgres goes further than you think. Measure before you migrate.
  3. Async replicas lag. Design read-your-writes from day one.
  4. Replication is not a backup. An untested backup is not a backup.
  5. Before anything destructive: hostname, a second pair of eyes, and a restore you've actually tested.

Your turn

Design challenge

Design the data layer for Brightcart Reviews: product reviews with star ratings, photos, "helpful" votes, and a per-product average rating shown on every product page (80k reads/s). Some viral products get 20k "helpful" votes per minute.

Which stores? How do you compute and serve the average rating? What happens to the row for a viral review?

Hints
  • Incrementing one counter row 20k times a minute is a lock-contention problem.
  • Does the average need to be exact and instantly up to date?
  • Photos don't belong in the database (see Incident 09).
What a strong answer covers
  • Reviews in a relational or document store. Photos in object storage, with only keys in the database.
  • Votes appended as events and aggregated asynchronously (or sharded counters), instead of UPDATE … SET votes = votes + 1 on one hot row.
  • A denormalized, cached average rating per product, recomputed periodically or incrementally.
  • Read-your-writes for the reviewer's own review, and eventual consistency for everyone else.
