Month 9 · Consistency Models

Split Brain

  • Role: engineer on the multi-region project
  • 3 decisions
  • ~18 min
Mon 09:00 #project-two-regions kickoff
Mon 09:01 @devin: West Coast users see 80ms of extra latency on every request. Plan: run Brightcart active-active in us-east AND us-west. Both regions serve reads and writes. Data replicates between them.
Mon 09:02 @morgan: "Both regions accept writes." Everyone remember that sentence. We'll be coming back to it.

Going multi-region means every piece of data now lives in two places 70ms apart. Every write raises a question: do we wait for the other region to confirm (slow, but always correct), or do we confirm locally and replicate in the background (fast, but the regions can disagree for a while)?

There's no single right answer. It depends on the data.

M

Morgan · Staff SRE · 14 years on-call

Junior engineers ask "should the system be consistent?" Seniors ask "what's the cost of this particular piece of data being wrong for 2 seconds?" For a profile picture, nothing. For the last pair of limited sneakers, a very angry customer. Decide per data type.

Decision 1 · Design review

How should the inventory count for limited-edition items work across two regions?

The sneaker drop has 50 pairs. East and West each see stock = 50 and each sell 50 before the replication messages cross. You've sold 100 pairs of a 50-pair item. Fifty apology emails go out, and a sneaker blog writes it up. Eventual consistency on a hard invariant means you'll eventually be wrong.

You picked strong consistency for the invariant and eventual consistency for everything else. 70ms on one button is invisible. Overselling isn't. For truly global hot items you could split the stock into per-region allocations (East gets 25, West gets 25, rebalanced periodically), so each region decrements locally without risk.

This is a legitimate business strategy, and airlines overbook on purpose. For Brightcart's regular stock with thousands of units, the rare oversell is cheap to apologize for. For a 50-unit limited drop, you'll cancel a large share of orders and wreck trust. Fine for some data. You need to know which.

Two weeks into active-active. A support ticket:

Thu 15:31 ticket #91402: "I changed my shipping address, the page said Saved, I refreshed and it showed my OLD address. Changed it again. Refreshed. Old again. Which one will my order ship to?!"

The user's write went to us-west (their nearest region). Their refresh was routed to us-east by a DNS change, and us-east hadn't received the update yet.

Decision 2 · Support escalation

How do you fix this without slowing down every read?

Session guarantees are the sweet spot. The user always sees their own writes and never sees time go backwards. Other users might see the old address for a second, which doesn't matter to anyone. You fixed what users actually experience without the cost of global strong consistency.

Correct, but every write now pays the cross-region round trip, about 70ms plus consensus overhead, and if the link between regions degrades, writes everywhere slow down or stop. You used a sledgehammer on a thumbtack.

"Working as designed" while orders ship to the wrong address. Users think in sessions: "I just changed it." Any design that lets a user see their own change disappear will look like a bug to them, no matter what the docs say.

Month 9, Sunday 22:52. Planned maintenance on the fiber link between regions.

22:52:10 network: us-east ↔ us-west link DOWN
22:52:27 db-failover: us-east primary unreachable from quorum → promoting us-west-db to PRIMARY
22:52:53 network: link UP (outage: 43s)
22:52:54 SEV1 TWO PRIMARIES: us-east-db accepted 1,214 writes during partition; us-west-db accepted 2,890 writes

For 43 seconds both databases believed they were the primary and accepted writes. There are 4,104 writes that the other side has never seen, including orders, payments and address changes. Some touch the same rows.

Decision 3 · 22:55 · Split brain

What now?

Site's back up in 10 minutes. Monday morning: 1,214 orders, payments and account changes have vanished. Customers were charged for orders that no longer exist. You turned a 43-second blip into a data-loss incident that finance spends a month cleaning up.

Server clocks in the two regions are 380ms apart. Last-write-wins silently picks the "later" timestamp, which isn't necessarily the later write, and throws away the other without telling anyone. Some inventory decrements are lost, so stock counts drift. It looks fine, and that's what makes it dangerous.

You chose data integrity over availability. It's a painful, slow, public night: the site is degraded for hours while the team replays East's writes into West, resolves the conflicts by hand or with business rules, and verifies. But no customer loses an order or a payment. Morgan: "That's the call GitHub made in 2018. It cost them 24 hours. It was still the right call."

Debrief

What you just learned

CAP, the honest version

When the network Partitions (and it will, as you saw for 43 seconds), each side has to choose:

  • Consistency: refuse requests that might be wrong (you froze writes in Decision 3), or
  • Availability: keep serving and accept that the sides will diverge (what the automated failover did).

PACELC extends it: when there's a Partition, choose A or C. Else, in normal operation, choose Latency or Consistency. Decisions 1 and 2 were the "else" half: 70ms of latency for consistent inventory.

The consistency ladder, strongest to weakest

ModelGuaranteeBrightcart example
Linearizable (strong)Behaves like one copy. Every read sees the latest completed writeInventory decrement, payments
SequentialEveryone sees operations in the same order (not necessarily in real time)Ordered event logs
CausalIf A caused B, everyone sees A before BA review reply never shows up before the review
Read-your-writesYou always see your own writesAddress change (Decision 2)
Monotonic readsYour reads never go back in timeRefreshing never shows older data
EventualIf writes stop, all replicas converge eventuallyReview counts, product descriptions

Quorums: tunable consistency

With N replicas, write to W and read from R. If R + W > N, every read overlaps at least one replica with the latest write. With N=3, W=2, R=2 you get strong reads and can tolerate one node down. With W=1, R=1 you get fast and eventual. Dynamo, Cassandra and Riak expose these knobs per request.

When replicas disagree: conflict resolution

  • Last-write-wins: simple, and silently loses data. Clocks lie.
  • Version vectors / vector clocks: detect that two writes were concurrent, and let the app merge them.
  • CRDTs: data types that merge automatically and correctly (counters, sets, text). Used in collaborative editors.
  • Avoid conflicts: give each record a single home region (Decision 1), so conflicts can't happen.

It happened for real

Real postmortems

GitHub · Network partition & MySQL failoverOct 21, 2018 · 24h 11m degraded

Routine maintenance replacing failing optical equipment cut connectivity between GitHub's US East Coast network hub and its primary East Coast data center for 43 seconds. In that window, their automated failover tool (Orchestrator, which uses Raft consensus) promoted MySQL primaries in the US West Coast data center. When connectivity returned, writes went to the West, but the East had accepted writes that hadn't replicated. GitHub chose to protect data integrity: they kept the site degraded while they restored and reconciled data, rather than discarding writes to recover faster. It lasted 24 hours and 11 minutes.

Lesson: automated failover can turn a 43-second blip into split brain. Decision 3 was modeled on this. Read the whole postmortem: it's one of the best ever published.

Source: GitHub: October 21 post-incident analysis

Amazon · Dynamo and the shopping cartSOSP 2007

Amazon's Dynamo paper describes a deliberate availability-over-consistency choice: "add to cart" must never fail, even during failures. Concurrent cart versions are merged when they're read. The documented side effect: an item you deleted can occasionally reappear. Amazon decided that's far better than ever losing an add.

Lesson: consistency is a business decision. Amazon chose AP for carts and would never choose it for payments. The skill is knowing which data can tolerate which anomaly.

Source: Dynamo: Amazon's Highly Available Key-value Store (PDF)

Field notes

Morgan's rules of thumb

  1. Choose the consistency level per data type, by asking what being wrong for 2 seconds costs.
  2. Hard invariants (stock, money) get one home. Everything else can replicate asynchronously.
  3. Session guarantees (read-your-writes, monotonic reads) fix most user-visible weirdness cheaply.
  4. Last-write-wins means "lose data silently." Don't trust wall clocks across machines.
  5. Automated failover needs fencing, so the old primary can't accept writes after a new one is promoted.

Your turn

Design challenge

Brightcart Wishlists go global: users in the US, EU and Japan, each served by their own region. Users add items from their phone and laptop, sometimes offline, and share lists with family members who also edit them.

What consistency model? What happens when two people edit the same list during a partition? What does the user see?

Hints
  • Is a wishlist more like a bank balance or more like a shopping cart?
  • "Add item" and "remove item" can commute if you model them right. Look up OR-Set CRDTs.
What a strong answer covers
  • An AP / eventually consistent design with automatic merging: a set CRDT, with adds and removes tagged with unique IDs.
  • Read-your-writes per device, plus offline queueing and sync.
  • An explicit choice about add/remove conflicts (add-wins vs remove-wins) and why.
  • A home region per list as an alternative, with the latency trade-off for remote editors.
