Month 2 · Caching

The Night the Cache Went Cold

  • Role: primary on-call
  • 3 decisions
  • ~15 min
23:41 infra: kernel patch rollout → redis-prod-1 rebooted (planned)
23:42 redis-prod-1 keys: 0 hit rate: 0.0% (was 97.8%)
23:43 postgres-primary cpu 100% active queries 1,912
23:44 SEV1 product-page p99 14.2s error rate 22%

Brightcart's product pages read through a cache-aside layer in Redis. On a cache miss, the app queries Postgres, writes the result to Redis with a 1-hour TTL, and returns it. Hit rate is normally 97.8%, so the database only ever sees about 2% of reads.

A planned kernel patch just rebooted Redis, and Redis came back empty. Now the database sees 100% of reads, about 50 times its normal load.

M

Morgan · Staff SRE · 14 years on-call

Here's the thing nobody tells you: the day your cache hit rate passes about 90%, the cache stops being an optimization and becomes load-bearing. Your database can no longer survive without it. We just found out the hard way. What now?

Decision 1 · 23:45

The database is drowning in cache misses. What's your first move?

Every app server reconnects to Postgres at once, so you've added a connection storm to a query storm. The restart also wipes each server's in-process local cache, the one thing that was still absorbing some load. p99 goes from 14s to timeouts.

When 5,000 users ask for the same hot product at once, coalescing (also called single-flight) sends one query and makes the other 4,999 wait for its result. Database queries drop by about 90% in a minute. The rate limit protects the database while the cache refills. Hit rate climbs back to 90% by 23:58.

Morgan: "That's the move. You fixed the shape of the load, not just its size."

Reasonable instinct, wrong timescale. Creating a replica from a snapshot and catching up takes about 40 minutes, and the new replicas start with cold buffer caches, so they're slow at first too. By the time they're ready, the Redis cache has mostly refilled on its own. Good long-term idea, useless in this incident.

Things recover by midnight. But reviewing the graphs the next morning, you notice something odd from before the incident: every hour, right on the hour, database CPU spikes to 85% for about 40 seconds.

09:00:00 postgres cpu 84% cache misses/s 11,400
10:00:00 postgres cpu 86% cache misses/s 11,900
11:00:00 postgres cpu 85% cache misses/s 11,700

The catalog warm-up job loads all products into Redis at deploy time, every key with TTL = 3600. So they all expire at the same second.

Decision 2 · Next morning

How do you fix the hourly spike?

The hourly spike becomes a daily spike, and a bigger one, because more traffic has piled up behind it. Meanwhile prices and stock counts can now be stale for up to a day. You traded frequency for size and correctness.

Jitter spreads the expiries across 20 minutes instead of one second, so the spike flattens into noise. Early refresh (sometimes called probabilistic early expiration) means hot keys almost never actually miss. Database CPU at the top of the hour: 31%.

Now every bug in your invalidation code becomes permanent stale data. Three weeks later, a product whose price changed during a failed deploy shows the old price until someone notices. Memory also fills up with products nobody views. A TTL is your safety net. Keep it.

Month 3. A support escalation: a customer bought a $129 jacket for $12.90. The merchandising team fixed a typo'd price within a minute, but the site kept showing $12.90 for another hour. You look at the write path:

// on price update
db.update(product)
cache.set(product.id, product)   // refresh cache

Two admins edited the price within milliseconds of each other, and the older write's cache.set landed last.

Decision 3 · Escalation

How should writes interact with the cache?

Deleting instead of setting means the cache never holds a value the writer thought was current. It only ever holds what a reader actually got from the database. This is what Facebook's memcache does. (A rare race remains, a slow reader repopulating stale data, which Facebook closes with leases. See below.)

Writes are now fast, but Redis is the source of truth for up to 30 seconds. The next time Redis reboots (you know it will), those price changes and any orders that relied on them are gone. Write-back is for data you can afford to lose: view counters, analytics. Never money.

Better consistency for the single-writer case, and reads are always warm. But two concurrent writers can still interleave and leave the cache disagreeing with the database. Plus every write now pays for two systems, and products nobody reads still fill the cache. Survivable, but it doesn't actually fix the race.

Debrief

What you just learned

The four caching patterns

PatternRead pathWrite pathUse whenDanger
Cache-aside (lazy loading)App checks cache; on miss, reads DB and fills the cacheApp writes DB, then deletes the keyDefault choice. Most web appsStampedes on cold start
Read-throughCache loads from DB on miss itselfUsually paired with write-throughWhen a library or proxy owns the cacheSame stampede risk, now hidden
Write-throughAlways warmCache and DB written synchronouslyRead-after-write heavy dataSlower writes, cache fills with unread data
Write-back (write-behind)Always warmCache first, DB later (async)Counters, metrics, loss-tolerant dataData loss if the cache dies

Eviction and expiry

  • LRU (least recently used): evict whatever hasn't been touched longest. A good default, and Redis's allkeys-lru.
  • LFU (least frequently used): evict the least popular. Resists one-off scans that flush hot keys out of an LRU cache.
  • FIFO: evict the oldest insertion. Simple and rarely optimal.
  • TTL: time-based expiry, your safety net against invalidation bugs. Always add jitter.

The stampede (thundering herd) toolkit

  • Request coalescing / single-flight: one database fetch per key, everyone else waits on it.
  • TTL jitter: never let a million keys expire in the same second.
  • Early or background refresh: refresh hot keys before they expire.
  • Leases (Facebook's memcache): on a miss, the cache gives one client a token to fill the key; others wait or get slightly stale data. Fixes stampedes and stale sets.
  • Serve stale on error: an hour-old price is usually better than a 500 page.
  • Capacity rule: once the cache is load-bearing, plan database capacity for a cold cache, or make sure the cache can never go cold all at once (replicas, persistence, gradual restarts).

It happened for real

Real postmortems

Facebook · "Worst outage in over four years"Sep 23, 2010 · ~2.5 hours

An automated system checked cached configuration values and, when it found an invalid one, replaced it from the database. Then someone changed the database copy to a value the system considered invalid. Every client saw the bad value and tried to "fix" it by querying the database cluster, hundreds of thousands of queries a second. Errors from the overloaded database were themselves read as invalid values, so clients deleted the cache key and retried, a feedback loop that kept the database down even after the original fix.

To break the loop, Facebook had to turn the site off, let the databases recover, then let users back in gradually.

Lesson: recovery logic that fires on every client at once is a stampede waiting to happen. Treat database errors as "back off", never as "invalidate and retry."

Source: Facebook Engineering: More details on today's outage

Facebook · Scaling Memcache (research paper)NSDI 2013

Facebook's paper on running memcache at the scale of billions of requests per second introduced leases to solve two problems you met tonight: stale sets (Decision 3) and thundering herds (Decision 1). It also explains why they delete on write instead of updating.

Lesson: the patterns in this module aren't theoretical. They were battle-tested at extreme scale and written up so you don't have to rediscover them at 23:45.

Source: USENIX NSDI '13: Scaling Memcache at Facebook

Field notes

Morgan's rules of thumb

  1. Past about 90% hit rate, the cache is load-bearing. Test what happens when it's empty.
  2. Delete on write, don't set. Let readers fill the cache.
  3. Every TTL gets jitter. No exceptions.
  4. Never use write-back for anything involving money.
  5. There are only two hard things in computer science, and cache invalidation is the one that pages you.

Your turn

Design challenge

Design the cache for Brightcart's "items in stock" badge. It appears on every product page, gets 80k reads per second, and during flash sales the stock count changes 500 times per second for a single hot product. Showing "In stock" when it's sold out causes angry customers. Showing "Sold out" when it isn't loses sales.

Which caching pattern? What TTL? How fresh is fresh enough, and does the answer change when stock is low?

Hints
  • Does the badge need the exact count, or just "plenty / few left / sold out"?
  • Tolerable staleness can be dynamic: 60 seconds when stock is 4,000, 1 second when it's 4.
  • The authoritative check happens at checkout anyway, so the badge can be a hint.
What a strong answer covers
  • Caching a coarse bucket rather than the exact count, so far fewer updates change the cached value.
  • A short or dynamic TTL, plus explicit invalidation when the item crosses a bucket boundary.
  • Request coalescing for the hot product, and a local in-process cache of about 1 second to absorb the 80k reads per second.
  • Correctness enforced at checkout (atomic decrement), not at display.
